Browser flight game · living design doc

Aloft.

A serene, melodic flight game about reading the air and staying up — closer to gliding over Hyrule at dawn than to Mario's cape.

v0.8 updated Jul 5 2026 phase 3 / a face and a voice title provisional

The feel

What it should feel like before it does anything

Serene, melodic, calming — with just enough challenge to stay engaged. The reference point is the first floating moment in Breath of the Wild, not the adrenaline of the cape. Earthy nature tones, flat-shaded low-poly with depth and heart, deliberately not photorealistic.

  • The tone test — calm, gentle, zen. The overarching tone of the whole game, decided Jul 3. Every mechanic, control response, visual, sound, and reward must pass it; when two tunings both work, ship the gentler one. Nothing snaps — inputs ease in, cameras drift, consequences soften.
  • No fail state. Landing is never a punishment. Touching water gently resets you to the nearest shore. The stakes are the ones you set yourself.
  • A slight challenge, never a wall. The skill is in reading the air and stringing lift together. Mastery is optional and always rewarded, never required.
  • The world teaches the wind. The scenery itself tells you where the lift is — proven in the Phase 2 playtest (flyable with everything hidden). Amended Jul 5: quiet instruments turned out to be a pleasure, not a crutch — so a calm minimal HUD (speed · altitude · a climb glow) ships as part of the game's face, while the world stays the primary teacher.
  • Pleasing first, deep second. If a flying cube over an empty plane already feels good, everything after is content.

The keystone mechanic

Both references are secretly the same equation

BotW's paraglider is descent management; Mario's cape is momentum rhythm. They feel opposite but they run on one idea — you trade altitude for speed and back again, while drag slowly bleeds the total and lift tops it back up. Build the whole game on this and both feelings fall out for free.

total energy = altitude + airspeed
— bleeding to drag, restored by lift

pitch down → faster + lower · pitch up → slower + higher (until stall)

One arcade-simplified flight model delivers the gentle survey-glide and a real skill ceiling for anyone who wants to pump for height. Phase 0 exists to answer one question about this and nothing else: does gliding feel good?

Reading the air

Lift you can see without a UI

  • Thermals. Rising columns over sun-baked ground. Telegraphed by shimmer, drifting dust or pollen, and birds circling overhead. Catch one and you climb without effort.
  • Ridge lift. Wind hitting a windward slope pushes up along the face. Real slope data (procedural or, later, real terrain) feeds this directly.
  • Ambient tells. Grass bend, drifting leaves, ripples on water, the lean of trees — the wind is legible everywhere, so the player learns it by feel rather than by reading a meter.

This is the system that turns "flying around" into "flying well." It's the first thing to build after the bare flight model proves out.

The core loop

Glide-and-explore, with rhythm-flight living inside it

Climb to height, launch, read the wind, reach a vista or an island, repeat in a slightly bigger world — the A Short Hike shape. The pure momentum-flow of the cape isn't a separate mode; it's simply what skilled play of the same energy model looks like. You never have to pump for altitude, but it's always there if you want it.

Goals stay loose and optional: a far rock you're not sure you can reach, collectibles that extend your glide or climb, beacons to light across an archipelago. The point is the flight, not the checklist.

Decisions

The calls that shape the architecture

The two real forks — music and session shape — are now settled (Jun 13). Q3 and Q4 are smaller calls made by default, still easy to overturn. Kept here as the record.

Q1

Music — adaptive, with a simple bed first

Adaptive music that responds to flight state — layers swelling as you climb, thinning on a dive, a motif when you catch a thermal — is the truest expression of "serene + melodic." The audio layer is designed to allow it from the start, but P0–P1 ship a simple ambient bed so it never blocks flight tuning.

decided → design for adaptive, ship a simple bed early
Q2

Session shape — infinite sandbox, arc on top later

A serene sandbox you can glide forever with self-set goals is the foundation, built so a small authored arc (reach the summit, chase one sunset to a resting place) can sit on top later without rework. Build the toy first; the arc is content.

decided → infinite sandbox foundation, arc layered later
Q3

World scale — bounded or streaming?

An infinite procedural world needs chunked terrain streaming and LOD; a single bounded valley fits in one mesh and is far simpler.

call made → start bounded, keep the terrain interface from precluding streaming later
Q4

Input — what do you fly with?

Keyboard/mouse, gamepad, or touch. Touch matters eventually (PULSE went there), but it changes feel-tuning.

call made → prototype keyboard/mouse, design input-agnostic; gamepad & touch as later adapters
Q5

Altitude regain — environmental lift, confirmed in the air

Three candidates were weighed. Environmental lift — thermals, ridge lift, vents — the Spider-Man 2 wind-tunnel shape: generously placed, strongly telegraphed, ride it up. The pump — dive to build speed, flare to climb — lives in the energy model as pure skill, physics-honest: it spends energy well but can't create it. Mario's cape bounce — cut: net-positive energy from the stick alone would make the world optional, which fights the whole fantasy. The Phase 1 build settled it — finding and using lift is satisfying (playtest, Jul 5).

decided → environmental lift primary · pump as skill layer · no bounce
Q6

How does a flight end?

Landing ends the session (noted Jul 5) — but as a chosen ending, never a fail. Two kinds of ground contact to separate: an accidental brush (skimming a hillside) should stay soft and recoverable, while a deliberate landing — settling onto a branch, a lakeshore, a rooftop perch, a light post — closes the flight like a resting breath. Perches double as destinations: places worth flying to. Ties into Q2's "chase one sunset to a resting place" arc idea. To flesh out: how intent is read (low + slow + near a perch?), what the landing moment looks and sounds like, and what a session summary shows (see the flight journal, parking lot).

leaning → deliberate landing = gentle session end · accidental contact stays soft · details in P4

The character

Whatever you fly is a readability instrument first, mascot second

From the chase cam it has to telegraph pitch and energy state at a glance. That quietly settles the question more than cuteness does. Deferred until flight feels good — but the bet is narrowing.

  • Soaring bird (hawk, albatross). Rides thermals diegetically; a true soarer justifies pure no-flap gliding, which is exactly the energy model. Banking looks gorgeous. Hardest to animate without jank.
  • Caped being. The cape is the readout — billows on a dive, snaps on a pull-up. Most direct line to the Mario reference, lowest effort to feel good (cloth on a few bones, not convincing creature flight).
  • Flying squirrel — cut. It's a glider, not a soarer; can't regain altitude. Fights the core loop.

For prototyping: fly a literal low-poly wedge. It reads pitch and bank with zero art. Decide bird-vs-cape after flight feels good. Current bet: soaring bird or caped being.

Scenery & terrain

Don't pick the scenery source — abstract it

The flight model and lift system don't care where the heightfield comes from. Build the sim against an abstract terrain provider, and "procedural" and "real-world elevation" become two implementations of one interface, swappable later.

Start procedural

Not because it's better long-term, but because it's the only source where you control lift placement — which is the entire question the early phases exist to answer. Real terrain is often flat and dull to fly, and it marries less cleanly to low-poly. Procedural first; real-world is a content mode you bolt on once flight already feels great.

The real-world idea, done right (later)

The dream version of "Google Maps scenery" is not the photoreal flythrough. It's: name a place → pull real elevation (USGS/SRTM or Mapbox/Terrarium tiles, mostly free) → render it in your own low-poly style → drop thermals on the sun-facing slopes for today's date. You fly the real shape of Yosemite, but it looks like your game. "Date" decouples cleanly into lighting — real sun angle for a lat/long and date, atmospheric and basically free.

Photoreal 3D tiles are explicitly out (see deferred): paid, heavy, and they fight the art direction.

Art direction

Earthy, flat-shaded, atmospheric

  • Flat-shaded low-poly with depth and heart — think Tiny Glade / Sail Forth, not photogrammetry. Cheap to render in the browser, and the look ages well.
  • Earthy nature palette. Mossy greens, warm sand and bark, stone greys, sky and dusk tones. Saturation kept calm.
  • Time-of-day as a free reward. A gentle day cycle gives you a sunset to chase without any extra systems.
  • Atmosphere over detail. Distance haze, soft fog, and light doing the emotional work — height should feel like height.

Tech & architecture

Same stack as EPOCH, same separation discipline

Vite + TypeScript + Three.js, flat-shaded low-poly, deployed through the existing nk00 pipeline — no new infrastructure to figure out.

Renderer-agnostic core

Mirror the EPOCH architecture so the parts that matter stay testable and swappable:

  • Sim core — flight model, energy, lift sampling. Pure logic, no Three.js. This is where "feel" lives and where you'll spend tuning time.
  • Terrain provider — an interface the sim queries for height + slope at a point. Procedural and real-DEM both implement it.
  • Lift provider — thermals and ridge lift, derived from terrain + sun + wind.
  • Three.js renderer — draws the world and the chase cam from sim state.
  • Audio layer — designed to allow adaptive scoring even while it ships a simple bed.
  • UI shell — HTML/CSS, minimal by design.

Phase gates

Each gate answers exactly one question

Run it like PULSE: nothing past a gate starts until the gate's question is answered yes. The early ones are deliberately tiny.

0

The wedge flies

A low-poly wedge over a flat plane. Energy model + chase cam. No terrain, no lift, no art.

does gliding feel good?
1

One hill, one thermal

Add a single shaped hill and one thermal + ridge lift. Prove you can gain and hold altitude by reading the air.

is finding and using lift satisfying?

Readable tells (dust, grass, birds), procedural terrain shaping, distance haze. A small bounded world worth exploring.

can a player feel the wind without a HUD?
3

It has a face and a voice

Commit to bird or cape. Layer in the adaptive-capable audio (simple bed first) and the day cycle.

does it have heart, not just physics?
4

A reason to keep flying

Loose optional goals on the sandbox foundation, persistence (settings + discoveries). The authored arc waits.

do you want to come back to it?

Deferred / out of scope for v1

Things we're consciously not doing yet

  • Photoreal 3D tiles. Paid, heavy, melts up close, fights the art direction. Out.
  • Real-world elevation mode. The good version of the Maps idea — but a content mode for after flight feels great, not a day-one data pipeline.
  • Mobile / touch & gamepad. Designed for via input-agnostic core; built later.
  • Multiplayer. Not now, maybe never. The vibe is solitary and calm by design.
  • The world-design trap. The pull to design the whole world before flight feels good. Resist it. One hill, one thermal, one landing.

plan · P-mobile  Touch controls via the stick guide (noted Jul 5): the on-screen virtual-stick circle (dot eases to the edge the harder/longer you push — shipped as a readout in the Phase 2 build) becomes the touch input surface on mobile: drag inside the circle to fly. One control metaphor across desktop and touch, already wired to the sim's input easing.
